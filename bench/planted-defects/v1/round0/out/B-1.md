Remove phrases such as:

- “Responsible for”
- “I worked on”
- “cutting-edge AI synergies”
- “drive innovation across the organization”

They do not communicate what you actually built or achieved.

Use action-oriented language:

- Designed
- Reduced
- Automated

- Evaluated
- Diagnosed
- Deployed
- Improved

### 4. Define unclear metrics

Several metrics are promising but need context:

- “Improved model performance by 35%”
- “Reduced backlog by 68%”
- “Improved claim correctness by 7%”
- “Evidence-citation quality by 9%”
- “Tool-use accuracy by 8%”

Clarify:

- What metric was used?
- Was the improvement relative or percentage-point?
- What was the baseline?
- What was the evaluation set or production period?

For example:

> Improved [F1/accuracy/latency] by 35% on [dataset or workload] through [specific intervention].

Avoid saying “improved by 7%” when you mean “improved by 7 percentage points.” Those are materially different.

### 5. Make the resume easier to scan

Your technical content is dense. Keep most bullets to one or two lines where possible. The current bullets contain valuable detail, but some read like research abstracts.

For example, this is technically impressive but very dense:

> Hardened a multi-agent fan-out/fan-in runtime against nested-pool deadlocks, reuse of incomplete streamed responses, and tool-result loss during context compaction...

Consider keeping the engineering details but shortening the structure.

Also fix the line-break hyphenation in the final document:

- `sensor signals`, not `sensor` line break `signals`
- `held-out`, not `held-` line break `out`
